What a realistic ad budget looks like for a small business

Forget “what do other businesses spend”. Work backwards from what a customer is worth and what the platforms need to learn.

VBy VladyslavPublished 8 September 20265 min read

“How much should I spend on ads?” rarely has a satisfying answer, because the honest one is “it depends”. But it depends on a few specific things you can work out yourself, and once you do, the right budget range becomes much clearer.

This guide walks through a practical way to set a budget: start from what a customer is worth, work out what you can afford to pay for one, then check whether that budget is enough for the ad platforms to actually learn. The numbers in the examples are illustrative, so swap in your own.

First: separate the three costs of advertising

When people talk about an “ad budget”, they often mix up three different things:

  1. Ad spend: what you pay Meta, Google or TikTok for impressions and clicks. This goes directly to the platform.
  2. Management: what you pay an agency or freelancer (or the value of your own time) to plan, build, optimize and report on campaigns.
  3. Creative: the cost of producing images, video and copy, either included in management or paid separately.

All three matter when you judge whether advertising is profitable. A campaign with cheap leads but expensive management, or a great agency with too little spend to learn anything, will both disappoint.

Work backwards from what a customer is worth

The most useful number in advertising is your allowable cost per customer: the most you can pay to win a new customer and still make money. You can estimate it in three steps.

1. Estimate customer value

Use the average value of a first purchase, or, if customers reliably return, a conservative estimate of what they spend over the first year. Be honest. If most customers buy once, use the first purchase.

2. Apply your margin

Revenue isn't profit. Multiply customer value by your gross margin (after cost of goods, materials or direct labor) to see how much profit a customer produces.

3. Decide how much of that profit you'll invest

You won't want to spend all of it on acquisition. Many businesses accept spending a portion of first-purchase profit, or even break even on the first sale, if repeat business is strong.

Illustrative example

A renovation business has an average job value of €4,000 and a gross margin of 30%, so each job produces about €1,200 in gross profit. The owner is happy to spend up to a third of that to win a job: an allowable cost per customer of roughly €400.

If about one in five qualified leads becomes a signed job, the business can afford to pay up to around €80 per qualified lead. That's now a concrete target to plan campaigns around.

Once you know your allowable cost per lead, the question “is this campaign working?” has a clear answer.

Make sure the budget is big enough to learn

Ad platforms optimize by learning from conversions. If a campaign generates too few of them, the algorithm can't tell who your best customers are, and results stay unstable.

Meta's own guidance is that an ad set needs around 50 optimization events within a week to exit its learning phase. Many small businesses won't hit that with purchases or signed contracts alone, which is why we often optimize toward an earlier step, such as a lead or a booking, while still tracking what happens next.

A rough sanity check: multiply your realistic cost per lead by the number of leads you need in a month to learn anything meaningful. If your expected cost per lead is €40 and you want at least 30 leads in the first month to judge results, you need around €1,200 in ad spend for that month. If your budget is far below that, you have three options:

  • Narrow the focus: one platform, one service, one location.
  • Optimize for a cheaper, earlier action (a lead form rather than a purchase).
  • Accept a longer learning period before judging results.

What doesn't work is spreading a small budget across many campaigns, platforms and audiences, where none of them gets enough data.

Budget in phases, not a single number

A realistic plan usually has three phases, each with a different job.

PhaseGoalBudget approach
Test (first 4–8 weeks)Find out which offers, audiences or keywords and creative produce leads at an acceptable cost.Enough to generate meaningful data on one platform. Expect results to be uneven.
StabiliseConsolidate around what worked; cut what didn't.Similar spend, concentrated on winners. Cost per lead should become more predictable.
ScaleGrow volume while keeping cost per customer under your limit.Increase in steps, adding new creative and new campaigns as you go.

Common budget mistakes

  • Judging after three days. Early results swing wildly. Make decisions on weeks of data, not days, unless something is clearly broken.
  • Constant budget changes. Large or frequent budget edits can push campaigns back into learning. Change budgets deliberately and in moderate steps.
  • Too many platforms at once. Two half-funded platforms usually perform worse than one properly funded one.
  • Ignoring what happens after the lead. If leads wait a day for a reply, your cost per customer rises no matter how good the ads are.
  • Copying someone else's number. A competitor's budget tells you nothing about your margins, your close rate or your capacity.

When to increase your budget

Increase spend when three things are true at the same time:

  1. Your cost per customer has been under your allowable limit for several weeks, not just a lucky few days.
  2. You can handle more leads without response times slipping.
  3. You have fresh creative or new campaigns ready, so extra budget isn't just showing the same ad to the same people more often.

When you do scale, do it gradually and watch cost per customer closely. It's normal for costs to rise a little as you reach beyond your most responsive audience. The question is whether they stay below your limit.

A one-page budget plan

Before your next campaign, write down:

  • Average customer value (first purchase or first year): ______
  • Gross margin: ______ %
  • Allowable cost per customer: ______
  • Expected lead-to-customer rate: ______ %
  • Target cost per lead: ______
  • Leads needed per month to learn: ______
  • Monthly ad spend required: ______
  • Management and creative cost: ______

If the numbers don't work on paper, they rarely work in practice. That's useful to know before you spend anything.
