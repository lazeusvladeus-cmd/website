We reviewed 132 marketing agency websites. Here’s what they show, and what they hide.

Fewer than 1 in 10 publish a price. Most lead with big numbers. Here’s what we found, and what it means if you’re hiring an agency.

VBy VladyslavPublished 4 October 20265 min read
Key takeaways
  • Only 12 of 132 agency homepages (about 9%) showed any price.
  • About 64% led with big aggregate numbers; 32 showed them without a case study or named testimonial.
  • Around 31% of headlines were slogans that didn't say what the agency does.
  • The best sites named an audience, an outcome and a concrete free first step.

When we rebuilt the Vike Marketing website, we wanted to see the market the way a business owner sees it: as someone with a budget, a problem and a browser full of agency tabs. So in October 2026 we reviewed 132 marketing agency homepages and recorded what each one actually showed a first-time visitor.

Some findings were expected. Others surprised us, mostly in what was missing. This article shares the numbers, our method and its limits, and what we think it means if you're the one choosing an agency.

How we did it

We looked at the homepages of 132 distinct agencies. The sample mixed well-known performance and PPC agencies from the US, Canada and Australia; digital agencies in the UK, Ireland and the Netherlands; agencies positioned for small and local businesses; and agencies in Ukraine and Eastern Europe serving international clients.

For each homepage we recorded objective, visible features: the headline, the main call to action, whether prices were shown, contract terms, types of proof (client logos, named testimonials, case studies with numbers, review badges), the first step offered, and the length of any visible form. Collection used an AI research tool that visited each page; we then read the notes and spot-checked surprising results.

Two honest limits. First, we only looked at homepages, so an agency with prices on a separate page counts as “not shown on the homepage”. Second, the data came in two batches, and the second batch was recorded more conservatively. Where the two batches disagree, we quote the more detailed sample of 74 agencies and say so. Treat the numbers as a reliable picture of the market, not a census.

1. Fewer than 1 in 10 show a price

Only 12 of the 132 homepages, about 9%, showed any price at all, and the figure was the same in both batches. Where prices did appear, they ranged from entry packages around €200 or £250 a month to retainers of several thousand a month, and one agency stated an average retainer of over $20,000 a month.

We understand why agencies avoid it: scope varies, and a price can scare off a prospect before a conversation. But from the buyer's side, it means you usually can't tell whether an agency is even in your budget range until after a sales call. If you're a small business, that's a lot of calls.

What to do as a buyer: ask for a ballpark monthly fee in the first email, and ask whether the fee is flat or a percentage of ad spend. A good agency will give you a range quickly.

2. “No long contract” is rarely stated

In our detailed sample of 74 agencies, about 15% said on the homepage that they don't require long-term contracts. That doesn't mean the rest lock clients in; many simply don't mention it. But contract terms are one of the first things small businesses worry about, and most homepages leave the question unanswered.

What to do: ask about the minimum term and the notice period before you get to a proposal, not after.

3. Big numbers everywhere, context rarely

About 64% of homepages made large aggregate claims: total revenue “driven”, total ad spend managed, total clients served. Big numbers signal scale, and some agencies back them with detailed case studies.

But 32 homepages showed aggregate claims without either a case study with numbers or a named testimonial anywhere on the page. In a few captured pages, animated counters even displayed as “$0” or “0+” because the animation hadn't run, which undermines the very proof they were meant to show.

What to do: a big total tells you little about your situation. Ask for one or two examples close to your size and industry: the starting point, the budget, the time frame and what actually changed.

4. Almost a third of headlines don't say what the agency does

Around 31% of headlines were slogans or abstractions rather than a description of a service, consistently across both batches. Lines like “Redefining possible” or “Find clarity in chaos” may suit a brand, but a first-time visitor has to scroll to find out what is being sold and to whom.

The clearest headlines named an audience and an outcome. Two examples we liked: “Marketing that actually books jobs for local service businesses” and “Run ads that drive pipeline and revenue for your B2B SaaS”. You know within seconds whether you're in the right place.

We also noticed a 2026 trend: several agencies now lead with visibility in AI answers, for example getting found “from Google to ChatGPT”. Expect more of that language as search changes.

5. The first step is often vague

Most homepages (about 80%) had a call to action in the opening section, but roughly 1 in 5 did not. The most common button texts were “Contact us”, “Get started” and “Let's talk”. They're clear enough, but they don't tell you what happens next.

The stronger examples named the first step and what you'd get from it: a free audit, a free proposal, a marketing plan or an “opportunity review”. In the detailed sample, about 43% offered something like this. Calendar booking directly from the homepage was less common, around 14% of the detailed sample.

What to do: if an agency offers a free audit, ask what it covers and whether it's written. A useful audit should be valuable even if you never hire them.

6. Forms often ask a lot, early

Only 28 homepages showed a form directly. Their median length was five fields, and 12 of them asked for six or more, often including budget, revenue or traffic. Qualification questions save the agency time, but they also put work on the visitor before any trust exists.

7. Few show the people behind the work

In the detailed sample, about 36% showed founder or team photos on the homepage, and only around 15% had an FAQ section. For a service where you'll work closely with people for months, it's surprising how often you can't see who they are, or find answers to the basic questions without booking a call.

What this means if you're hiring an agency

  • Ask for a price range and fee model in your first message.
  • Ask about minimum terms and notice periods up front.
  • Ask for one or two relevant examples with real context, not totals.
  • Prefer agencies whose first step gives you something useful in writing.
  • Find out who will actually run your account, and talk to them.

Our longer guide to choosing a marketing agency covers these questions in more depth.

What we changed on our own site

It would be odd to publish this and ignore it ourselves. So the new Vike site publishes all three plan prices, states month-to-month terms, names a concrete free funnel audit as the first step, keeps the booking form to two fields, answers common questions on every main page, and shows only testimonials from real clients, with no counters or totals we can't back up.
