How to choose a marketing agency without getting burned

The questions to ask before you sign, the red flags that should end the conversation, and what a good first month actually looks like.

VBy VladyslavPublished 18 August 20265 min read

Most small business owners who have hired a marketing agency have a story about one that didn't work out. Reports full of impressions and “reach”. An account manager who changed every two months. A contract that took longer to exit than the campaigns took to fail. And at the end, no clear answer to the only question that mattered: did this make us money?

The good news is that most bad agency relationships are predictable. The warning signs show up in the first conversation, if you know what to listen for. This guide covers what to work out before you start looking, the questions worth asking, the red flags that should end the conversation, and what a healthy first month looks like.

Start by knowing what you actually need

“We need marketing” is too vague to hire against. Before you talk to anyone, try to answer three questions in a sentence each:

  • What outcome do you want? More booked appointments, more online orders, more qualified project enquiries, more enrollments. Pick the one that pays the bills.
  • What is a new customer worth to you? Even a rough number (average first purchase, or average value over a year) changes the whole conversation about budget.
  • What can you handle? If twenty extra leads arrived next week, who would call them back, and how fast?

These answers help you filter agencies quickly. An agency that never asks about them is planning to sell you activity, not results.

Questions to ask every agency

You don't need to be a marketer to evaluate a marketer. You need to ask direct questions and pay attention to how directly they're answered.

Who will actually work on my account?

Many agencies sell with senior people and deliver with junior ones. That isn't automatically bad, but you should know who is setting strategy, who is building campaigns, and who you will talk to when something goes wrong. Ask how many other accounts that person manages.

Who owns the ad accounts, pixels and data?

The only acceptable answer is: you do. Your Meta Business Manager, Google Ads account, Google Analytics property and tracking should be created under your ownership, with the agency added as a partner or user. If an agency runs your ads from its own account, you can lose years of data and learning when you leave.

How do you report, and what do you report on?

Ask to see a sample report. Look for metrics that connect to money: cost per lead, cost per booked appointment, cost per purchase, revenue or pipeline value. Impressions, reach and clicks are useful diagnostics, but if they're the headline, be cautious.

How do you charge, and do you mark up ad spend?

Your ad spend should go directly from your card to Meta or Google. The agency's fee should be separate and clearly stated. If they handle the spend themselves, ask whether any margin is added on top.

What's the minimum commitment?

Some longer engagements make sense, especially for SEO where results take months. But for paid ads, a long lock-in before an agency has proved anything puts all the risk on you. Month-to-month or a short initial term with a clear review point is a reasonable ask.

What would make you turn us down?

This is the most revealing question. A good agency has a clear view of when it can't help: margins too thin, no capacity to handle leads, a product it doesn't understand. An agency that would take on anyone is an agency that can't be selective about doing good work.

Red flags that should end the conversation

  • Guaranteed results. Nobody controls the ad auction, Google's rankings or your competitors. Promises of a specific number of leads, a guaranteed ROAS or “page one in 30 days” are sales tactics, not plans.
  • Vague answers about ownership. If they hesitate when you ask who owns the ad account, move on.
  • No questions about your business. If the proposal could have been sent to any business in any industry, it probably was.
  • Case studies without context. A big percentage increase means little without the starting point, the budget, the time frame and what actually changed. Ask what the business was and what the agency did.
  • Pressure to decide today. Discounts that expire at the end of the call are a sign of a sales process, not a partnership.
  • Everything at once. A small business rarely needs six channels on day one. An agency recommending everything is usually recommending the biggest invoice.

How agency pricing models compare

There are three common ways agencies charge for ad management, and each has trade-offs.

ModelHow it worksWatch out for
Flat monthly feeA fixed fee for a defined scope, separate from ad spend.Make sure the scope is written down: platforms, creative volume, reporting frequency.
Percentage of ad spendThe fee is a percentage of what you spend on ads.It rewards the agency for spending more, not for spending well.
Performance-basedThe agency is paid per lead or a share of revenue.Can encourage chasing cheap, low-quality leads. Lead definitions matter enormously.

For most small businesses, a flat fee with clear scope is the easiest to budget and the least likely to create bad incentives. At Vike we use flat monthly plans for exactly that reason, and they're listed openly on our pricing page.

What a good first month looks like

A competent agency's first few weeks should look roughly like this:

  1. An audit. A written review of your current ad accounts, tracking, website and competitors, with honest findings, including what's already working.
  2. Tracking fixed before spending. Conversion tracking verified so the platforms optimize for real leads or sales. This is the most commonly skipped step, and the most expensive one to skip.
  3. A documented plan. Which campaigns, which audiences or keywords, which creative angles, what budget, and how success will be judged.
  4. Your approval before launch. You should see the ads before they run.
  5. Early, honest updates. The first weeks of a campaign are about learning. A good agency tells you what it's seeing and what it's changing, without overreacting to two days of data.

Small agency or large agency?

Large agencies bring more specializts and established processes. Small agencies usually bring direct access to the person doing the work, and more flexibility. For most small businesses, the deciding factor is attention: who will actually think about your account each week? Ask, and judge the answer.

A short checklist before you sign

  • You know who will work on your account day to day.
  • All ad accounts and tracking are owned by you.
  • The fee, the scope and the ad spend are clearly separated in writing.
  • You've seen a sample report, and it talks about leads, sales or revenue.
  • You know how to end the agreement, and it doesn't take a lawyer.
  • They asked you more questions than you asked them.
