Meta Ads vs Google Ads: which should a small business start with?

Google captures demand that already exists. Meta creates it. Here's how to tell which one your business needs first.

VBy VladyslavPublished 27 August 20265 min read

“Should I run Facebook ads or Google ads?” is one of the most common questions we hear from small business owners. It's a fair question, but it's usually framed the wrong way. The platforms aren't competing versions of the same thing. They do different jobs, and the right starting point depends on how your customers find and choose businesses like yours.

This article explains the core difference, how to compare their costs without fooling yourself, and a simple framework for deciding where your first marketing budget should go.

The core difference: capturing demand vs creating it

Google Ads captures existing demand. Someone types “emergency plumber near me” or “buy trail running shoes size 44”. They already know what they want. Your ad appears at the moment of intent, and your job is to be the most convincing answer.

Meta Ads (Facebook and Instagram) creates demand. Nobody opens Instagram looking for a dentist. Your ad interrupts someone scrolling, and your job is to make them notice a problem, desire or opportunity they weren't actively thinking about, then act on it.

That single difference explains most of what follows: why the costs look different, why the creative requirements differ, and why some businesses thrive on one platform and struggle on the other.

When Google Ads usually makes sense first

  • People already search for what you sell. If there is clear search demand (“accountant in [city]”, “roof repair”, “private school near me”), you can put yourself in front of it from day one.
  • Needs are urgent. Emergency services, repairs, legal help, urgent medical or dental care. When someone needs a solution now, they search; they don't wait for an ad to find them.
  • Your offer is hard to show visually. B2B services, professional services and technical products often suit text-based search ads better than image-led social ads.
  • You have a strong landing page. Search traffic converts on the page. If you have a clear, fast page that explains the offer, Google can work quickly.

When Meta Ads usually makes sense first

  • Your product or service is visual. Food, fashion, beauty, fitness, home renovation, events, anything people can see and want.
  • It's new, or people don't know to search for it. If you've created a new product or category, there may be little search volume yet. Meta lets you introduce it to people who fit the profile.
  • Purchase decisions are emotional or impulse-driven. Gifts, books, experiences and lifestyle products often sell on feeling first, research second.
  • You can produce lots of creative. Meta rewards a steady flow of fresh images and video. If you can capture real content from your business, you have an advantage.

How to compare costs fairly

The most common mistake is comparing Google's cost per click (CPC) with Meta's cost per thousand impressions (CPM) or cost per click. Clicks on Google often cost more than clicks on Meta, which can make Meta look cheaper. But a click from someone actively searching for your service is not the same as a click from someone who was scrolling past a friend's holiday photos.

The fair comparison is further down the funnel:

  • Cost per lead: what does each enquiry, booking or form submission cost?
  • Lead-to-customer rate: what share of those leads actually buy?
  • Cost per customer: the number that matters, which combines the two.

It's common for Meta to produce cheaper leads that convert at a lower rate, and for Google to produce more expensive leads that convert at a higher rate. Only your own data, tracked through to sales, tells you which is better for your business.

The work behind each platform

The two platforms demand different kinds of effort, and it's worth being honest about which you can sustain.

A simple decision framework

Answer these four questions:

  1. Do people search for what you sell, in your area or market? Check Google's Keyword Planner or simply type your service into Google and see whether competitors are advertising. If yes, Google is a strong candidate.
  2. Can you show your offer visually in a few seconds? If yes, Meta is a strong candidate.
  3. Is the need urgent or planned? Urgent favors Google. Planned or impulse purchases can go either way.
  4. What can you produce consistently? Strong landing pages favor Google. A stream of photos and video favors Meta.

If you clearly lean one way, start there with a focused budget. If the answers are mixed, start with the platform closest to the sale (usually Google for service businesses, Meta for consumer products), and add the other once the first is producing predictable results.

Why most businesses end up using both

Once each platform is working on its own, they reinforce each other. Meta introduces your business to people who weren't looking; some of them later search for you by name on Google. Google captures active searchers; Meta retargets the ones who visited without converting. Running both lets you reach people at different stages of deciding.

The key word is once. Splitting a small budget across two platforms from day one often means neither gets enough data to learn. It's usually better to do one platform properly, then expand. Our guide on realistic ad budgets covers how to size that first budget.

What to do this week

  • Search for your main service on Google and note which competitors advertise.
  • Check the Meta Ad Library to see what competitors run on Facebook and Instagram.
  • Write down what a new customer is worth to you, and the most you'd pay to acquire one.
  • Make sure you can track leads or sales from your website before spending anything.
